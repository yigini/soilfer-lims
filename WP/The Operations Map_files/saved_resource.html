The Operations Map

The map you have draws the right picture of the wrong thing. It shows a sample's position in a fixed floor plan, which a technician already knows, and it never changes while you watch it. What a laboratory needs from a map is time: where the days are going, and which one sample out of four hundred needs someone today.

The red light is always red

Your screenshot shows SLA CRITICAL · 208d 5h elapsed. That is not a stale demo record — it is the model. workflowEngine.buildMapState computes risk like this, for every sample, whatever was ordered on it:

const hours = Math.floor((Date.now() - new Date(createdAt).getTime()) / 3600000);
sla = { totalHours: hours,
        severity: hours > 72 ? 'CRITICAL' : hours > 24 ? 'WARNING' : 'OK' };

Seventy-two hours, globally. Air-drying alone takes 24 to 72 hours before anyone touches the sample. Pipette sedimentation takes a day. Ammonium-acetate CEC is a multi-day procedure. Every sample that has actually been analysed is CRITICAL by construction, so within a week the badge means nothing, and staff stop reading it. An indicator that is always on is worse than no indicator: it trains people to ignore the place where real alarms will appear.

There is a second problem in the same three lines. createdAt comes from auditLog.find(e => e.action === 'SAMPLE_CREATED' || e.action === 'STATUS_CHANGE') — the first match in whatever order the log arrived, so two loads of the same page can produce different elapsed times.

01 What is actually there

More than it looks. The server already builds a proper map-state contract, the graph renders with a real flow library, and a working realtime hook exists elsewhere in the app. The gaps are specific.

MAP-A

The map is a snapshot, not a live viewCritical

useSampleWorkflow.js is 71 lines: two axios.get calls on mount, no subscription, no polling. Meanwhile hooks/useRealtimeData.js already implements WebSocket event subscription and is used by ManagerQueue and TechWorkbench. The server already broadcasts WORKITEM_CHANGED, WORKITEM_UPDATE and SPECTRAL_UPDATE.

So the one page in the product whose entire purpose is to show current state is the one page that does not update. That is also why there is a refresh button in the toolbar.

MAP-B

Nine of the thirteen workflow components are never importedCritical

Roughly 64 KB of React with zero import sites: WorkflowNode (19.9 KB), LabFloorPlan, WorkflowTimeline, WorkflowKPICards, WorkflowFilters, WorkflowExport, WorkflowDetailPanel, WorkflowEdge, WorkflowStageLane.

They are dated 15 February; the four live ones were rewritten on 1 September. So a v1 map was built, a v2 replaced it, and v1 was left in the tree. Several of them — WorkflowTimeline, WorkflowKPICards, LabFloorPlan — are the features being asked for now. Read them before deleting them; delete them either way.

MAP-C

The seven stations are hardcoded pixel positionsHigh

SampleWorkflowMap.jsx:269–280 places Reception, Preparation, Physical Testing, Chemical Analysis, Spectral Lab, QA Review and Archive at fixed coordinates in a three-row grid, for every sample.

Your screenshot shows the consequence: Physical Testing — Station on Standby and Spectral Lab — Station on Standby, drawn at full size, for a sample that has no physical or spectral analyses ordered. Two of the seven cards are furniture. A map that shows rooms this sample will never enter is teaching the floor plan, not the sample.

MAP-D

Room assignment falls through to Chemical AnalysisHigh

ROOM_MAP in workflowEngine.js:465 is a hand-maintained table of about forty analysis codes, and resolveRoom ends || 'Chemical Analysis'.

Every analysis not in that list — including all 214 the catalogue seeds — silently appears in Chemical Analysis. No warning, no flag, and the map looks correct while being wrong. Same defect as WP-25: the workflow graph is a second, divergent copy of the catalogue.

MAP-E

The same work is counted in two stationsHigh

Header says PROGRESS 0/7. Preparation shows 2 queued, Chemical shows 0/5 — that is the seven. QA Review then shows 5 Queued and 0/5 as well. The five chemical determinations are rendered at two stations, so the map implies twelve pieces of work where there are seven.

Review is a state of a work item, not a room it moves to. Drawing it as a room forces this double count.

MAP-F

There is no view above a single sampleHigh

The only route is /samples/:id/map. A laboratory manager's real questions are "where is my bottleneck this morning" and "will the Guatemala batch make its deadline" — neither is answerable one sample at a time, and a lab running 400 samples cannot open 400 maps.

MAP-G

Blocked exists in the engine but not as a state anyone can setMedium

buildMapState computes a blockerGraph from analysis prerequisites — good, and more than most systems have. But there is no way for a technician to say waiting for reagent, instrument down, or awaiting client authorisation. Those are the delays a manager can actually act on, and they are invisible.

MAP-H

The sample sits at a station with no ownerMedium

HANDLER · Unassigned with Next: Assign Drying task. The suggested next action is the best thing on the page — it should be the model for the whole design. But nothing escalates: a sample can sit unassigned at a station indefinitely and only appears on this map, which nobody is looking at.

MAP-I

Presentation details that read as unfinishedMedium

The minimap renders as an empty grey placeholder. Connector handles are visible on every card edge as decorative dots. The edges from Preparation to the three analytical stations are faint dashed grey and read as disconnected. Roughly 60 % of the canvas is empty at default zoom.

02 The idea: put time on the horizontal axis

This single change turns the map from a diagram into an instrument. A floor plan answers "which room" — which the technician standing in the room already knows. A time axis answers "where did the days go", which nobody currently knows.

Keep the node graph — demote it. The station diagram is genuinely useful for one thing: showing which determinations run in parallel and what blocks what. That is a route view, and it belongs as a secondary tab beside the timeline, drawn only for the stations this sample actually visits.

03 Three views, one page

Not three pages. One map with a scope switch, because the three questions are the same question at three zoom levels, and a manager moves between them constantly — floor, then batch, then the one sample that is late.

Scope · Sample

The journey

Timeline as above, with the route graph as a second tab. Every determination, when it started, who has it, how long it has been there, what it is waiting on, and the target it is measured against.

Only the stations this sample visits. Review shown as a state, not a room.

"Where did the days go, and what do I do about it?"

Scope · Laboratory

The floor, right now

Every open sample, grouped by station. Queue depth, oldest item, work in progress against capacity, and the technicians on shift. Stations sized by load so the bottleneck is the biggest thing on screen.

This is the view that runs on a wall display and opens the morning huddle.

"Where is the jam this morning?"

Scope · Project or batch

The cohort

A project's samples as a cumulative flow: how many have cleared each station over time, the spread of turnaround, and the projected completion date against the deadline.

The arrival-versus-completion gap is the honest measure of whether the laboratory is keeping up.

"Will the Guatemala batch land on time?"

Shared behaviour across all three

ElementBehaviour
LiveUpdates arrive over the existing WebSocket. A changed node flashes once and settles — no re-layout, no scroll jump, no full refetch.
ActionableEvery node offers what can be done to it from where you are: assign, reassign, waive, place on hold, print labels, open the bench.
SelectableRubber-band or click-select many samples at a station, then act on all of them at once. Assigning thirty samples to one technician is the daily job.
FilterableProject, matrix, analysis, assignee, status, age, overdue-only. Filters belong to the scope and survive a reload.

Legible from three metresA kiosk mode with larger type and no chrome, for the wall screen. This is what makes it a fixture rather than a page people visit once.

04 Turnaround, modelled properly

Everything above depends on one number being meaningful: how long this sample should take. Until that exists, every view shows red and none of them helps.

Where the target comes from

Each analysis in the catalogue carries a working-day turnaround. The sample's target is the critical path through its ordered analyses plus preparation and review — not a sum, because determinations run in parallel, and not a constant, because a pH-only sample and a full fertility package are different commitments.

ExampleTypical working daysWhy
Air-dry, mill, sieve2–3Drying is the method, not a queue. It must not count as delay.
pH, EC1Fast, high-throughput, usually batched.
Organic carbon, total N2–3Combustion runs are batched; digestion adds a day.
Available P, exchangeable bases3–4Extraction plus instrument time, batched.
CEC by ammonium acetate4–5Saturation, washing and displacement over several days.
Particle size by pipette3–5Dispersion and timed sedimentation cannot be hurried.
MIR or vis-NIR scan1Minutes of instrument time; the queue is the constraint.
Review and certificate1–2Manager review, QC disposition, report assembly.

Indicative, for shaping the model. Each laboratory sets its own values during onboarding — the same per-lab defaults mechanism as WP-20.

When the clock runs

RuleEffect
Start at ACCEPTED, not at record creationA sample logged in advance of arrival is not late before it exists — the current cause of much of the 208 days.
Count working days against the laboratory calendarWeekends and public holidays are not overruns. Seven national laboratories, seven calendars.
Stop on holdAwaiting client authorisation, awaiting reagent, instrument down, awaiting reference material. Held time is recorded and shown, but is not counted against the target.
Restart on reanalysis, and keep bothThe customer-facing clock keeps running; a second internal clock records the rework. Hiding rework flatters the laboratory and teaches nothing.
Stop at the certificateNot at archive or disposal. Disposal is housekeeping and must not colour the operations view red.

Three states, and they must be earned. On track comfortably inside target · At risk projected to breach on current pace · Breached past target with the clock stopped for holds. If more than about one sample in ten is red on a normal day, the targets are wrong, not the laboratory — and that is a signal worth surfacing to the manager during the first month.

05 What makes it feel alive

"Realtime" is not a WebSocket connection. It is a set of guarantees about what the viewer sees, and the current broadcast layer meets few of them.

GuaranteeTodayNeeded
Events carry enough to update without refetching { sampleIds, workItemId, status } — the client must refetch or ignore A typed patch: entity, id, changed fields, actor, server timestamp, monotonic version
Nothing is missed across a reconnect No sequence number; a dropped socket loses every event in the gap silently Per-connection cursor; on reconnect, replay since the last version seen
Late events cannot overwrite newer state Last message wins Version compare; drop anything older than what is rendered
Only relevant events arrive broadcastToLab sends every change to every user in the lab Subscribe by scope — this sample, this station, this project — so a wall display is not parsing four hundred irrelevant messages
The viewer can tell live from stale Nothing; a dead socket looks identical to a quiet laboratory A connection state and a last-updated stamp, always visible
Actions feel immediate Full refetch after every mutation Optimistic apply, reconcile on the echoed event, roll back visibly on rejection

The unglamorous half of "wow". A map that animates beautifully and quietly drops an event during a reconnect is worse than one that reloads honestly, because people will trust it and be wrong. Sequence numbers and replay are what make the polish safe to enjoy.

07 What not to build

Each of these is tempting, demonstrates well, and costs more than it returns.

Not thisWhy
A scale drawing of the physical laboratory with benches and doorsExpensive per laboratory, wrong for seven different floor plans, and it answers "which room" — the question staff already know the answer to. LabFloorPlan.jsx is the abandoned attempt.
Animated samples travelling along the edgesReads beautifully in a demo and adds nothing at 400 samples. Spend the motion budget on the one flash that says this changed.
A 3D or isometric viewOcclusion, no ordering, no measurable axis. Every serious operations board is flat for a reason.
Predicted completion by machine learningA median of the last thirty samples through that station is nearly as good, explainable to an auditor, and available immediately.
A drag-and-drop workflow editor on the mapThe route comes from the catalogue (WP-25). Two places to edit a workflow means neither is authoritative.
Free-panning infinite canvas as the defaultFit to content on load. An operations view should never open on empty space — as the current one does.

One test for the whole feature. Put it on a screen in the laboratory for a week and see whether anyone looks at it unprompted. If the answer is no, the problem is almost never the visual design — it is that the turnaround model is not trusted, or the data is not live. Those are Stages A and B, which is why they come first.
